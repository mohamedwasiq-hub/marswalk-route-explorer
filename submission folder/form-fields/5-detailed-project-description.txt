WHAT IT DOES

MarsWalk is a web map of a 4.6 x 4.0 km area on the Jezero Crater delta front on Mars. It has three tabs.

Compare:
- Route A, a direct line, and Route B, a terrain-aware path, go from the crater floor to the delta top.
- Each route gets a Terrain Difficulty Index (TDI) from 0 to 100, where lower is easier, plus its length, total climb, steepest point, average slope, share of ground over 15 degrees and data coverage.
- "What matters most?" sliders (gentle terrain, shorter distance, data coverage) change the ranking live.

Inspect:
- An elevation profile coloured by slope and linked to the map.
- Click any 60-metre segment, on the map or the profile, to see its slope, climb and a plain-language reason for its score.
- The route's three hardest stretches are listed one click away.

Plan:
- Draw your own route (Route C) by clicking waypoints. It is scored with exactly the same method.
- A plain-language route briefing for a crew member.
- JSON and CSV export.

On the map:
- A HiRISE image, hillshade, elevation and terrain-slope layers, with a legend.
- Science stops with sources.
- A live readout of latitude, longitude, elevation and slope.
- A Whole Mars view showing where Jezero sits on the planet.
- Data & method, Guide and Limitations panels are always one click away.

WHAT WE FOUND

The direct route is 2.26 km long but crosses ground up to 22.0 degrees, with 7.4% of it steeper than 15 degrees. The terrain-aware route is 12% longer (2.53 km), but it never goes above 14.1 degrees and climbs 17 m less. With the default weights it has the lower TDI (12.4 against 14.2). If only distance matters, the direct route wins, and the app shows that openly.

HOW IT WORKS

We read the NASA HiRISE elevation file (PDS format) directly, average it to a 3 m grid, correct the east-west pixel spacing for latitude, and compute slope. Route B comes from an A* least-cost search that avoids slopes over 25 degrees and prefers gentle ground. Every route is sampled every 6 m. The TDI combines slope, extra distance and data gaps, using weights the user controls. It is our own prototype formula, not a NASA standard, and the app says so.

HOW WE CHECKED IT

14 automated checks pass:
- test surfaces with known slopes;
- route lengths;
- missing-data handling;
- values against the raw NASA file;
- a hand calculation;
- a comparison with Horn's method, used by GIS software (median difference 0.21 degrees).

A sensitivity test showed the terrain-aware route beats the direct route under all five router settings we tried.

BENEFITS AND GOALS

MarsWalk makes route trade-offs visible and checkable, instead of hiding them behind one "best route" label. Planners can test their assumptions, and students can learn how terrain shapes exploration.

Next we want to:
- add rock abundance, thermal and radiation layers, including Emirates Mars Mission atmosphere data;
- model elevation uncertainty;
- add sunlight and line-of-sight to a base;
- test the scores against Perseverance's real drive paths.

LIMITATIONS

MarsWalk is a research and education prototype. It does not certify any route as safe. It looks at terrain only. It does not cover:
- rocks under 3 m;
- sand or dust;
- radiation, lighting or communications;
- spacesuit limits.

The slope thresholds are illustrative. The elevation model comes from 2007 images, and nothing has been tested in the field.

TOOLS

- Python 3 with NumPy (reading the NASA data, slope, routing, tests) and Pillow (images).
- JavaScript, HTML and CSS front end with the Leaflet map library, bundled so it works offline.
- Hosted on Netlify. Code on GitHub: https://github.com/mohamedwasiq-hub/marswalk-route-explorer
